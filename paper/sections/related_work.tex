\paragraph{Optimization in Deep Hashing} 
\blue{Besides W-type regularizers,}
alternative strategies either introduce auxiliary hash codes with a 
smoother surrogate $\|\cdot\|_2^2$~\cite{liDeepSupervisedHashing,
li2017deep,liFeatureLearningBased2016}, or refine the discretization 
objective via central similarity targets~\cite{yuanCentralSimilarityQuantization2020}.
\blue{Recent architectures retain squared W-type penalties: MambaHash~\cite{he2025mambahash}
and CRH~\cite{yin2026crh} use $\bz\mapsto\lVert|\bz|-\mathbf{1}\rVert_2^2$
to reduce quantization error.
Nevertheless, theoretically grounded methods for nonsmooth W-type regularization
composed with nonlinear network mappings remain limited. This is the gap we aim to address in this work.}

\paragraph{Primal-Dual and Stochastic Splitting Methods} Primal-dual methods, such as PDHG~\cite{chambolleFirstOrderPrimalDualAlgorithm2011} and its unified extensions~\cite{esserZhangChan2010}, are foundational tools for composite optimization with broad applications in imaging sciences. These methods enjoy ergodic $\mathcal{O}(1/k)$ convergence and remain stable under adjoint mismatch~\cite{chouzenouxAdjointMismatch}. Stochastic counterparts, notably SPDHG~\cite{chambolleSPDHG2018} \blue{and stochastic majorization-minimization subspace methods~\cite{chouzenouxStochasticMM2017}}, handle structured finite-sum objectives with almost-sure and ergodic convergence. In the nonconvex setting, Clason et al.~\cite{clasonAccelerationGlobalConvergence2019} established global convergence under the Kurdyka--{\L}ojasiewicz (KL) condition, while Guo et al.~\cite{guo2023preconditioned} further addressed nonconvex stochastic composite problems. More recently, Shumaylov et al.~\cite{shumaylovweakly} incorporated weakly convex neural-network regularizers into a primal-dual framework and proved convergence of PDHGM to critical points under the KL condition. When the problem admits a block-separable structure, splitting methods such as ADMM with its stochastic variants~\blue{\cite{ADMMNolinear2024,chen2025convergence}} and stochastic alternating \blue{minimization~\cite{driggs2021stochastic,InertialProximalAlternating,bian2026stochastic}} \blue{have been applied} to nonconvex multi-block optimization.

\paragraph{Stochastic Nonconvex Constrained Optimization}
From a constrained optimization perspective, Problem~\eqref{Eq: the general nonlinear composite problem in intro} \blue{can be reformulated via a variable lifting technique~\cite{wang2025augmentedrnn,jin2025multicomposite} as a nonlinearly constrained optimization problem of the form
\begin{equation}
  % \label{Eq: the constrained reformulation}
  \min_{\bw}\; f(\bw) + h(\bw)
  \quad \text{s.t.}\quad
  \bc(\bw) = 0. 
  \notag
\end{equation}}Several recent works have established oracle complexity results for first-order stochastic augmented-Lagrangian and penalty methods for problems of this type. For instance, Jin and Wang~\cite{jin2022stochastic} proposed a single-loop stochastic primal-dual method based on a linearized augmented-Lagrangian function, achieving oracle complexities of $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-6})$ from an arbitrary initial point and $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-5})$ from a feasible one for locating an $\varepsilon$-KKT point. By incorporating the recursive momentum estimator of~\cite{cutkoskyMomentumBasedVarianceReduction2019}, Shi et al.~\cite{shi2025momentum} developed a momentum-based linearized augmented-Lagrangian method, improving these bounds to $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4})$, or the nearly optimal $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})$ when initialized at a nearly feasible point. To certify a stronger notion of stationarity that deterministically enforces constraint violation of at most $\varepsilon$, Lu et al.~\cite{lu2024variancereducedfirstordermethodsdeterministically} introduced a linearized quadratic-penalty method with truncated momentum. Under a regularity condition on the constraint gradients, recursive momentum yields the nearly optimal complexity $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-3})$.

\noindent\textbf{Notations.}\quad Throughout this paper, we use $a$, $\ba$, and $\bA$ to denote scalars, vectors, and matrices, respectively.
\blue{All vectors are column vectors. For a matrix \(\bA\in\RR^{m\times d}\), we write \(\bA=[\ba_1^\top;\dots;\ba_m^\top]\), where \(\ba_i\in\RR^d\) and \(\ba_i^\top\) is the $i$-th row of \(\bA\).}
\blue{$\bm{1}$ denotes an all-ones vector or matrix of the appropriate size}, and $i,j$ serve as indices. For matrices \(\bA,\bA^{\prime}\in\RR^{m\times d}$, we use the Frobenius inner product \(\langle \bA,\bA^{\prime}\rangle\) and the induced Frobenius norm \(\|\bA\|:=\sqrt{\langle\bA,\bA\rangle}\). Unless specified otherwise, $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm for vectors and the Frobenius norm for matrices. $|\cdot|$ denotes element-wise absolute value.
\blue{For a matrix \(\bA=[A_{ij}]\), $\|\bA\|_1:=\sum_{i,j}|A_{ij}|$ denotes the \blue{componentwise} \(\ell_1\) norm.} For a closed set \(\calC\subseteq\RR^q$, the distance from \(\bx$ to \(\calC$ is $\text{dist}(\bx,\calC):=\min_{\by\in\calC}\|\bx-\by\|$.
\blue{The sign function is applied \blue{componentwise}, with $\operatorname{sgn}(t)=1$ for $t\ge0$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(t)=-1$ for $t<0$. For $\lambda>0$, $\operatorname{clip}$ denotes projection onto $[-\lambda,\lambda]$.}
